Heart rate: green on the finger. Red and IR are for SpO2. Auto: the ring's gain control (bright LED + offset DACs); moving the brightness or pulse sets manual mode. The LEDs flash briefly (25 times per second): IR is invisible to the eye and too dim for most phone cameras in normal use. Camera check: the selected LED at maximum for 6 s, look through the phone's front camera (rear cameras usually filter IR). To check the ring's heart rate, you can try to measure it with the webcam heart rate monitor (very experimental).

Each beat is timed on the green pulse (steepest edge, refined by matching the average pulse shape), only while the pulse is clear and the hand still; deformed beats and intervals off by more than 20 % from the recent ones are dropped. RMSSD (beat-to-beat changes) and SDNN (spread) over the last minute. At 24 samples per second the timing noise is ~7 ms per interval: an estimate, which reads a bit high when the variability is low.

Not calibrated, not a medical measurement: ratio of the red and IR pulses (each divided by the light that went through the finger) with the textbook curve 110 − 25 R. Most of the light reaching the sensor leaks inside the ring: the leak calibration measures it (kept in this browser, per ring and pulse length). The ring shows a value only when the red and IR pulses match (correlation ≥ 90 %). Wear the ring with the sensor on the palm side, hand palm up and relaxed, nothing pressing on the sensor: palm down on a table, the pulses were ~2.5× weaker.

From the frequency of an oscillator inside the microcontroller (RC16M, measured against the crystal every second): fewer counts when warmer, 5 to 8 counts per °C depending on the ring. The temperature of the ring itself, not of the skin. Each ring needs its own references: LibRing_6B, 0E, 06 and 57 come calibrated (22 and 37 °C); for another ring, "Set" adds a reference once the reading has settled at a known temperature (kept in this browser), two references far apart give its slope.
